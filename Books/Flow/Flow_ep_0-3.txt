Flow

Their feelings rise slowly, inexorably — like water filling a vessel. Every drop brings closer the moment when everything overflows: the silence, the glances, the unspoken words. Nika and Vika both know: one movement, one sound, and the fragile balance will shatter. But what will happen when the flow finally breaks free? And can anything stop it?

Prologue

Every other day we went for water — to the utility room, to the sink. The director never ordered delivery, so we walked.

The utility room sat at the end of the corridor, past the fire exit. A small room: a sink, a shelf of cleaning supplies, a fluorescent lamp humming under the ceiling. The door was heavy, with a rubber seal. When it closed, the sounds outside vanished completely. No air conditioner, no phones, no voices from the office. Only the hum of the lamp.

Nika would set her bottle under the tap. I would set mine. We turned the taps at the same moment. There came a quiet gurgle — two identical streams.

And then she would come to me.

Not right away. First she stood by her bottle, watching the water level rise. Then she came to me. Three steps. She stood beside me. Close. So close I could feel the warmth of her arm, though she never touched me.

And she looked into my eyes.

I looked back.

Not a word. Not a movement. Only the murmur of the streams against the plastic. Only the fluorescent light that made her face pale and finely cut. Only her eyes — dark, motionless — in which I could not read a single thought. But I could see that she was thinking *something*. Something she kept silent about.

Five litres take a long time to fill.

The seconds stretched. I counted them by the splashes: every bubble of air, every small strike against the bottom. It seemed to me that if I moved, everything would break. If I said one word, it would break. If I looked away, it would break. We stood in that silence like a room with no doors.

Sometimes she tilted her head, just barely . As if she wanted to see me from another angle. Or was about to speak — and changed her mind. Or was drawn to me, and held that pull back.

I was drawn the same way.
To raise my hand.
To touch her cheek.
To lean in and catch her breath on my lips.
To take the step neither of us had ever taken.

I didn’t.
And she didn’t.
And the streams went on murmuring.

Once she stood even closer. So close I felt her breath — warm, even — on my cheekbone. Her eyes were ten centimetres from mine. I saw myself reflected in them — tiny, upside down. And something else, besides — something I found no name for then, and find no name for now.

My hand was resting on the edge of the sink.
Then she raised her own — slowly, as if unaware of what she was doing — and took a lock of her hair. She wound that lock around my wrist. Once. Twice. Silently. Without a word.

I watched the dark threads against my skin — fine, almost translucent in the harsh light. I felt every single strand. The pulse beneath them seemed deafening.

And I did not pull my hand away.

Then her bottle filled. The water rose to the neck and the sound changed — denser, duller. I turned to the sink. Closed the tap. Her hair slid from my wrist as I reached for the plastic.

“Done,” I said.

“Done,” she said.

We went out. The door closed. The sounds of the office poured back in — the air conditioner, a keyboard, someone’s voice on the phone.

We returned to the office, and everything between us was as it had been before. 


Except for one thing: on my wrist, where her hair had lain, the warmth did not fade for several minutes yet.
