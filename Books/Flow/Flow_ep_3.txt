Flow

Their feelings rise slowly, inexorably — like water filling a vessel. Every drop brings closer the moment when everything overflows: the silence, the glances, the unspoken words. Nika and Vika both know: one movement, one sound, and the fragile balance will shatter. But what will happen when the flow finally breaks free? And can anything stop it?

Chapter 3: A LOCK OF HAIR

It was around three. The office was quiet — everyone at their monitors, keyboards tapping evenly, like raindrops on a roof.

I was looking at a layout. Nika beside me — at her own screen. My hand lay on the desk between us — an ordinary thing, the way it always was.

Then I felt it.

Not a touch. Not a breeze from the air conditioner. Something else entirely — light, weightless, like breath. A lock of Nika’s hair. It lay on my wrist — simply lay there, its tip against my skin, and I could not understand when it had arrived. A second ago it had not been there.

I did not move.

Nika did not move either. She looked at her monitor. She typed. An utterly ordinary young woman doing utterly ordinary work. Except that her lock of hair lay on my skin, and from that place a warmth spread up my arm — thin, fine, like the note of a tuning fork you hear only from the inside.

One second.

Ten seconds.

I glanced at the colleagues at the far end of the room. Sergey — in his headphones. Marina — drinking coffee, eyes on her phone. No one was turned toward us.

Half a minute.

The lock of hair lay on. The skin beneath it burned — not painfully, not hotly — it simply *burned*, the way a candle burns in a dark room: quietly, evenly, inevitably. I felt every millimetre of that touch — a weight barely perceptible, a pressure almost imaginary, and yet real. As real as my pulse, which I could now hear in my wrist — right there, beneath the lock of hair.

A minute.

Olga from accounting rose from her desk. I went still. She walked past us to the water cooler — didn’t look, didn’t turn her head. Poured herself some water. Left.

The lock of hair was not withdrawn.

My breathing grew shallow. I was afraid my chest would rise too visibly, that someone would see something was wrong with me. But I kept looking at my monitor. Kept sitting there. As usual.

A minute and a half.

Somewhere a phone rang. Someone laughed. The printer began to run — a long document, the rustle of pages.

The lock of hair lay on.

I thought: if someone turned around now, what would they see? Two colleagues. Side by side. Working. A lock of hair on a wrist — what is that? Nothing. Absolutely nothing. It means nothing. It’s just… hair.

But I knew it meant everything.

Two minutes.

Nika turned her head toward me — slowly, as if she wanted to ask something about the layout. She looked into my eyes. And I saw it — she knew. She was reading every breath I took. She felt what was happening beneath her lock of hair as precisely as I felt it.

Her eyes were dark. Calm on the surface. And not calm at all underneath.

She turned back to her monitor.

Another ten seconds.

And the lock of hair was gone. It simply vanished — she shifted slightly, and the lock of hair slid from my skin, leaving only the absence of its warmth, which I felt more keenly than the warmth itself.

I looked at my wrist. No mark. But I knew that this small patch of skin would remember. Would remember forever.

“The indent is wrong here,” Nika said in an ordinary voice, nodding at my screen.

“Yes, I see,” I said in an ordinary voice.

And we went on working.

Every time, something new.

And on the days when she did nothing, it was worse.

I would come to work and wait. Every movement of hers — whether she turned, or leaned over, or fell into thought — I read as a signal.

By lunchtime my nerves were stretched to their limit. By evening they would let go, and I would be angry with myself for the disappointment.

For three weeks she went on like this. One small step, a pause. One small step, a pause.
